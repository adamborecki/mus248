## 🎤 Lecture: Professionalism, the Core Skill Areas, and Audio/Video Basics

### Slide 83: Professionalism = Reliability

- **Be on time.** For recording work, "on time" usually means ready to work at the scheduled time, not walking in at that time.
- **Communicate early.** If something changes, tell the appropriate person before it becomes a problem.
- **Stay engaged.** Phones and unrelated distractions make you less useful to your group and make it harder to learn.
- **Take care of the equipment.** Set it up carefully, troubleshoot thoughtfully, and return everything where it belongs.
- **Help your group succeed.** Recording engineering is collaborative—don't disappear, let someone else do everything, or wait passively for instructions.
- **Practice basic hygiene.** You'll often be working closely with other people in small rooms and booths. Please shower, wear clean clothes, and use deodorant.
- **Finish the job.** Recording isn't finished when the performance ends: files need to be saved, checked, transferred, organized, and equipment reset.
- **Be someone people can trust.** Technical skills matter, but reliability and professionalism are what make someone hireable.

**MUS 248 goal:** Become the kind of engineer I can trust to handle a recording without needing me there.

### Slide 85: Audio Basics

- **Know your signal flow.** Sound source → microphone → preamp/audio interface → DAW → output/headphones/speakers.
- **Set the correct I/O.** Make sure the DAW is using the intended input and output device, then confirm the track is listening to the correct input channel(s).
- **Arm the right track.** Record-enable the track you want and confirm that you see meter activity before recording.
- **Use phantom power when appropriate.** Condenser microphones usually require 48V phantom power; dynamic microphones generally do not. Do not supply 48V to ribbon mics.
- **Set gain before recording.** Use preamp gain to get a healthy signal without clipping.
- **Avoid clipping.** If the signal reaches or exceeds 0 dBFS, it clips and distorts. A useful starting target is often around -12 to -6 dBFS peaks, but we might want smaller peaks for classical music to be safe.
- **Check mono vs. stereo.** Know whether you are recording one channel or two, and make sure stereo inputs are routed/panned correctly.
- **Listen, don't just watch meters.** Headphones can reveal distortion, noise, hum, bad routing, bad cables, and other problems.
- **Record a short test first.** Record → stop → play it back → verify that you actually captured what you expected.
- **Save deliberately.** Know where your session and audio files are being stored before making an important recording.

**Basic rule:** Before the real recording starts, you should know where the signal is coming from, where it is going, whether the level is appropriate, and whether it is actually being recorded.

### Slide 87: Video Basics

*Slide 86 is an earlier, shorter draft of this slide.*

- **Set exposure manually.** Avoid letting the camera constantly change settings during a performance. Lock in aperture/iris, shutter speed, and ISO/gain.
- **Know the exposure triangle.** Aperture/iris affects brightness and depth of field, shutter speed affects brightness and motion blur, and ISO/gain affects brightness and image noise.
- **Set frame rate intentionally.** Know whether you are recording at 24, 30, 60 fps, etc., and make sure it matches the project/workflow.
- **Match shutter speed to frame rate.** A common starting point is approximately double the frame rate (for example, 30 fps → around 1/60 sec).
- **Use ISO/gain carefully.** Raising ISO/gain makes the image brighter but can also add visible noise.
- **Set white balance manually.** Choose an appropriate color temperature (Kelvin) and keep it fixed so the color does not shift automatically during the performance.
- **Check focus.** Make sure the subject is actually sharp. Use autofocus or manual focus intentionally rather than assuming the camera got it right.
- **Check exposure visually.** Make sure important subjects are not blown out or buried in darkness; use the camera's meters/tools when available. – "important stuff" is correctly exposed: example: fingers or faces of musicians. Not solid black of a piano, outside of window
- **Check framing.** Make sure the important subject is actually in the shot and that the composition makes sense for the performance. – wide enough so that they don't go out of the frame when they take a bow
- **Record a short test first.** Record → stop → play it back → verify exposure, focus, color, framing, audio, and that the file actually recorded.

**Basic rule:** Before the real recording starts, you should know your frame rate, shutter speed, aperture, ISO/gain, white balance, focus, and framing — and those settings should not be changing automatically unless you intentionally want them to.
